Post-Astra Robotics

Frontier coding agents now lead a robot manipulation benchmark. Some thoughts on why, and on what that leaves for robotics research.

I started writing this blog post in Austin during CoRL 2026. As an undergrad attending CoRL for the first time, I spent a lot of time listening to discussions among PhD students and senior researchers.

On November 9, the first day of the main conference, Jim Fan's keynote had a slide about GPT-6 Astra, and within half an hour a clip of it was going around X. CoRL also ran its first-ever call for late-breaking results this year, with agentic robotics as one of the two topics. The same day, Anthropic reported that Claude Fable 5.2 scored 64.9% on RoboDojo Sim. People started saying that the Bitter Lesson was coming for robotics.

Whatever area of robotics people worked in, agentic robotics had become the elephant in the room, something we couldn't ignore.

Fable 5.2's score puts it ahead of every learned policy on the RoboDojo leaderboard (Fig. 1). Anthropic hasn't released per-task results, so for most of this post I use the closest open data I could find. Agent as Policy (AGP) runs GPT-6 Astra inside a coding-agent harness and released all 2,100 of its RoboDojo episodes, logs and videos included. It scores 63.6, about where Fable 5.2 is.

This is an impressive result, but I don't think it means robotics is solved, or that it came out of nowhere. Most of the excitement I've seen treats it as a surprise. I think it follows fairly directly from what the benchmark measures and from what robotics already knew how to do, and working through why also shows what is left to work on.

§1Manipulation benchmarks now test the brain

Newer manipulation benchmarks ask for more than motor skill. RoboDojo, which most of this post draws on, is a good example. Among its 42 simulated tasks, the robot has to arrange number blocks into a correct equation, play tic-tac-toe, or swap blocks around while keeping track of which is which.

In the equation task the robot has to read the digits, work out the arithmetic, decide which block goes where, then find those blocks and move them. Only that last part is really about motor control.

Today's VLAs have learned impressive action distributions; they fold laundry and open drawers they've never seen before. What they're missing is something like a brain. A VLA maps recent observations and an instruction to the next chunk of actions, and there's nowhere in that loop to hold a plan, track hidden state, or catch an arithmetic mistake. Tasks like these mostly test that missing piece, so they say little about how good a policy's hands are.

A frontier LLM approaches these tasks from the other direction. It's already good at reasoning, memory and breaking a task into steps, and what it needs is a way to turn its decisions into motion.

§2The Revival of Old-Fashioned Robotics

Before deep learning, robotics already had most of the pieces these tasks need. Given an object's pose, you can sample grasps, solve inverse kinematics for the arm, plan a collision-free path and track it with a feedback controller. These methods are mostly hand-designed, and they work across objects and arms.

They could handle long tasks, too. Shakey planned multi-step actions with STRIPS in 1971. In the late 1980s, Lozano-Pérez's Handey system planned pick-and-place motions, grasps included, from a geometric model of the scene. RRT (LaValle, 1998) made motion planning practical for high-dimensional arms, and task-and-motion planning (Kaelbling and Lozano-Pérez, 2011, among many others) chained symbolic and geometric plans over many steps.

The bottleneck was human engineering. Every new task needed someone to model the scene and write the state machine by hand: which object to pick first, where it goes, what counts as done, what to do when a grasp slips. A system built for one setup usually needed more engineering to work in the next one. That cost is a big part of why the field moved toward end-to-end learning, which replaced hand-written structure with data.

A coding agent takes over most of that engineering. It looks at the scene, writes a program that calls perception, IK and the planner, runs it, looks again, and changes the program if something went wrong (Fig. 4). A VLA has to learn failure recovery from data, while an agent gets a basic version of it by checking its own work.

Ken Goldberg made a similar argument in September, in an essay titled “Goosebumps”, where he calls agentic robotics “a third path between two dominant cultures in robotics.” I came into robotics through the learning culture, so it was a useful reminder of how much the other one still does.

Let's look at an example from RoboDojo. AGP released the full event log for every episode, minus the model's private reasoning. Fig. 5 replays one, the solve-equation task, which the agent finished in twelve tool calls.

Every command here is the model's own output, worked out in its reasoning, but it is almost never a joint command: across all 2,100 episodes it sent about a hundred joint-space moves, against roughly 50,000 end-effector targets. It works in pixels and poses and leaves the joints to IK and the planner. In Fig. 5, before releasing the piece, the one step it can't undo, it measures again and works around a depth estimate it doesn't trust. Across the dataset, about one tool call in five reports a failure the agent has to handle.

The AGP prompts also include a few hand-written hints per task, such as how wide to open the gripper before picking up a nut, so some human engineering is still in the loop, written as text instead of code.

The same stack also explains where the agent fails. On the hands tasks in Fig. 2 it scores 0% on inserting a key and filling an egg holder and 4% on plugging in a charger. These depend on contact forces that a camera can't see, and the old tools were never good at contact either.

§3Thinking in seconds

The objection I hear most often is about time. In the AGP logs an episode takes 8.6 minutes for 24.5 seconds of robot motion, and about six of those minutes are the model thinking rather than simulation or planning (Fig. 7). The model alone would have to get about 14× faster to keep up with the robot.

Faster chips, smaller models and models that need fewer tokens for the same answer each cut into that, together something like 3–15× a year. At that rate the agent catches up with the robot somewhere between late 2027 and early 2029.

Even then, some reasoning stays on the critical path, since recovering from a mistake means looking at the result and deciding what to do next. Brooks argued in 1986 for fast reactive layers that don't wait on a planner, and the three-layer architectures that followed (Gat, 1998) put a slow deliberative layer on top of a skill sequencer and a fast controller. The agent fits in the top layer: it thinks while a skill runs and steps in when the skill goes off track.

I don't expect the LLM to take over the fast layer as well. A general model takes over a layer once it gets under that layer's latency, and I expect that to happen first in the middle of the stack, the pick-and-place decisions VLAs make today at a few hertz. Closing a contact loop at hundreds of hertz needs roughly a thousand times more speedup than keeping pace with the robot, and the same hardware gains make a small robot model faster too.

Speed still matters a great deal. A small model with the same reasoning would make these agents cheap and fast enough to run on real robots, and that would already change how robots are deployed. It wouldn't fix contact, though. The agent is just as slow on swap blocks, where it scores 96%, as on insert key, where it scores 0%. Knowing where the key goes doesn't tell you how to feel it into the slot. So the fast layer stays a separate, smaller model, though the agent may be the one that builds it, setting up the simulator, collecting the data and training the policy it will later call.

§4Moravec's paradox, again

In 1988 Hans Moravec pointed out that the abilities we think of as intelligent, like arithmetic and chess, are comparatively easy for computers, while the perception and motor skill a one-year-old has are hard. RoboDojo reads like the same observation almost forty years later: the agent solves the equation and then can't insert the key.

For robot learning this is good news. High-level reasoning is becoming something you can get from an API, which takes away problems that teleoperation data was never going to solve well: arithmetic, memory, task decomposition, knowing what a charger is for. What's left is the part Moravec called hard, and it is now much easier to see.

It also means learned policies aren't going away. NVIDIA's GR00T 2, presented at CoRL the same day, splits the work along this line. On its own it scores 35.5% on RoboDojo. With RL post-training and run as a tool under a frontier agent that plans the task, watches progress and repairs failures with code, it reaches 75.8%, the highest reported score, though on one of RoboDojo's three layout banks. On real robots the same split takes three cluttered assembly tasks from 71–84 successes out of 100 to 97–98.

§5What we can work on

If the paradox holds, most of the work left in manipulation sits below the agent. These are the problems I think are still open.

I think this lets roboticists be more ambitious than ever. Until recently, a project usually had to choose between the brain and the hands: planners handled long tasks in simplified worlds, and learned policies handled short motor skills like folding a shirt or opening a drawer. With reasoning available from an API, we can work on tasks that need both, like assembling furniture from its manual, cooking from a recipe in a kitchen the robot hasn't seen, or repairing a machine, where the plan is long and the hard steps are contact-rich. These are also the tasks that would matter outside the lab.
